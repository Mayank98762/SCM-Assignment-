### 2.2 Derived observations. These already shape the thesis.

## 3. PHASE 3: Research strategy and verification protocol

**Hierarchy:** (1) APSEZ annual report and investor presentation FY26, plus Q1/Q2 FY27 filings on NSE/BSE → (2) MoPSW, IPA and Gujarat Maritime Board / state documents; Major Port Authorities Act 2021; Indian Ports Act 2025 (whether it was enacted and its tariff provisions are **to verify**); MoPSW "Maritime Amrit Kaal Vision 2047" → (3) credit-rating rationales (CRISIL/ICRA/Moody's/S&P/Fitch), which give an independent view on leverage, concession risk and cargo concentration → (4) broker research (ICICI Direct, others) and business press (ET, Business Standard, Mint, Reuters where accessible) → (5) peer-reviewed strategy and port-economics literature.

**Rules applied from the outset:**
- Each figure gets a data-log line with its value, unit, period, source ID, verification status (Primary-verified / Secondary-consistent / Single-source / Conflict) and a guidance-vs-actual flag.
- Company claims are kept apart from independent evidence. "Market leader", "largest", "integrated" and similar labels stay as *company-reported* until an IPA or MoPSW total confirms them.
- FY, calendar-year and quarterly figures are never combined in one ratio.
- Known conflicts are logged now and not smoothed over: owned trucks (937, ICICI Direct) vs "25,000+ trucks" (owned plus managed, secondary); vessel count 129 (Q3 FY26) vs 136 (FY-end); JSW capacity 177 vs 183 MTPA.

**Independent calculations to run (so the analysis doesn't simply repeat company figures):**
- Domestic market share = APSEZ domestic cargo ÷ (major + non-major ports total from MoPSW). This needs the March 2026 non-major report. The cumulative April–February FY26 figure is 690.77 MMT (provisional).
- Capacity utilisation = **domestic** cargo ÷ **domestic** capacity. The 500.8 MMT figure includes international volume and must not be divided by the 653 MTPA domestic capacity.
- Segment EBITDA margins FY24–FY26, and ROCE by segment where it is disclosed.
- Organic growth = growth excluding NQXT and other acquisitions made during the year.

### 4.2 VRIN plan
- **Basis:** Barney (1991). VRIN is used as the *attribute screen*, and the analysis concentrates on the **N** criterion, which VRIO drops. The substitution tests will be concrete:
  - Can shippers serving north-west India reach the same hinterland through JNPA, Vadhavan (when commissioned), Pipavav or Hazira over the Western DFC?
  - Can Indian transshipment shift to Colombo, Vizhinjam or Vallarpadam? APSEZ owns two of those three, which is a hedge in itself.
  - Can coastal shipping or rail replace port-to-hinterland flows?
- **Imitability:** analysed using Dierickx & Cool (1989): asset-mass efficiencies and time-compression diseconomies explain why a 25-year build cannot be bought quickly. Historical conditions (Barney 1991) cover first-mover access to private-port policy in Gujarat.
- **Output:** a matrix of 10 resources × V/R/I/N, with a ✓/partial/✗ rating, evidence and the vulnerability for each cell.

### 4.3 VRIO plan
- **Basis:** Barney (1995) and Barney & Hesterly (textbook edition to confirm). VRIO's **I** (costly to imitate) replaces VRIN's I+N, and **O** is added.
- **Evidence for O:**
  - Organisational structure: segment reporting, the Adani Logistics Ltd / Ocean Sparkle / Astro subsidiaries (to verify which ones exist and are relevant).
  - Integration of acquired ports (volume and margin trajectory).
  - Commercial organisation (bundled port-plus-logistics contracts).
  - Board and governance (independent directors, related-party approval process; NQXT bought from a promoter-group entity with majority-of-minority approval).
  - Capex discipline (guidance vs actual).
- **Classification:** parity / temporary advantage / sustained advantage / *unexploited advantage* (V-R-I yes, O weak). The last category is the most likely candidate for logistics.
- **§5.3 reconciliation table:** each resource gets its VRIN verdict, its VRIO verdict, the reason they differ, and which one is more defensible.

### 4.5 Porter's Five Forces plan

**Industry boundary (decided before rating):** commercial cargo-handling port services in India, broken down by **cargo segment** (container / dry bulk / liquid) and **catchment** (north-west hinterland, west coast south, east coast). The forces differ by segment: container buyers (shipping alliances) are much stronger than captive bulk buyers. The headline ratings will refer to the core segment, with the variation shown.

| Force | Research questions and evidence | Initial lean (to be tested) |
|---|---|---|
| Rivalry | Number and capacity growth of rivals (major ports 915.17 MT in FY26; JSW 122 MT, ~183 MTPA; DP World; Vadhavan); landlord-model PPPs at major ports; excess capacity (Haralambides 2002) | Moderate. Rising on the west coast. |
| New entrants | Capital intensity, concessions, land, environmental clearance, draft; but state-backed entry (Vadhavan) and well-funded private entry (JSW) | Low–moderate. Barriers are high, yet the state can lower them. |
| Supplier power | Cranes (concentrated, often Chinese OEMs: a security and policy angle), dredging, land, labour, concession grantor as "supplier" of the right to operate (the decisive supplier) | Moderate. **High** for the concession grantor at renewal. |
| Buyer power | Shipping alliances (Gemini, MSC, Ocean), larger vessels, cargo owners; switching costs for bulk with dedicated conveyors and long-term take-or-pay contracts vs containers | Container: high. Bulk: low–moderate. |
| Substitutes | Few substitutes for sea trade itself. Substitution is between gateways and routes: the DFC shifts the hinterland; overseas transshipment hubs; coastal shipping; pipelines for liquids; and long-run decline in thermal coal | Low overall, moderate for specific cargoes. |

The diagram will be the five-force layout with a rating and the 2–3 strongest pieces of evidence beside each force. An attractiveness verdict then follows ("moderately attractive industry; APSEZ out-earns it because…"), kept separate from the firm-level findings.

## 5. PHASE 5: Integrated analysis design

### 5.1 Central argument (provisional, to be tested and possibly rejected)

> APSEZ's durable advantage is a **network of long-tenure concessions anchored on India's north-west coast and tied into the hinterland**. Rivals cannot copy it quickly (time-compression diseconomies), and it lets APSEZ earn domestic-port EBITDA margins above 70% in an industry where several forces sit at moderate or high. That advantage is **legally contingent** (Mundra's concession runs to 2031), **concentrated** (≈82% of FY26 EBITDA comes from domestic ports), and **diluted in returns** as growth shifts toward lower-margin logistics and marine businesses and capital-heavy overseas market development. The open strategic question is therefore whether APSEZ's organisation and capital-allocation discipline can turn a location-and-licence advantage into a network advantage that holds up through concession renewal and new state-backed capacity. Whether APSEZ is advantaged *today* is not in doubt.

### 5.2 Hypotheses that link the frameworks (each to be confirmed or rejected)

| H | Link | Test |
|---|---|---|
| H1 | VRIO (RS1/RS3) → Five Forces: location and concession advantages offset container buyer power | Do APSEZ realisations per tonne and margins hold against JNPA/JSW? Did market share stay flat while volumes grew? |
| H2 | VRIN-N → threat: the DFC and Vadhavan weaken non-substitutability for Mundra's NW hinterland | Hinterland distance/cost analysis; Vadhavan timeline |
| H3 | VRIO-O → Ansoff: logistics expansion is an "unexploited/temporary" resource, not a sustained one | Logistics margin trajectory; evidence of bundling |
| H4 | Ansoff market development is driven by resource transfer (operating know-how) rather than by location advantage, so it carries more risk | International EBITDA margin (28.6%) vs domestic (~73%) |
| H5 | Scale is a *temporary* advantage, because rivals can match capacity within a decade, whereas *network breadth plus concessions* is the sustained one | Compare capacity trajectories |
| H6 | Governance and related-party risk sits outside the five forces but directly limits the "capital access" resource | Rating actions and spreads around the 2023 and 2024 events |

### 5.3 Synthesis diagram (planned)
A left-to-right flow: **Resources (VRIN/VRIO verdicts)** → **pressures they offset or fail to offset (Five Forces)** → **growth vectors they can support (Ansoff)** → **recommendations**. Edges are colour-coded by strength of evidence.
